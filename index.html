Bond markets, explained simply

A bond is a fancy IOU.

When a government or a company needs money, it borrows from people like you. You hand over cash today. They promise to pay you a little "thank you" money along the way, and give your cash back on a set date. That promise, written down, is a bond.

Why bonds exist

Why not just print money or sell shares?

Borrowers have three ways to get cash. Bonds are often the least painful.

Risky

Print money

Only governments can, and too much makes every dollar worth less. Prices go up for everyone. That's inflation.

Costly

Sell shares

Only companies can. Each share sold gives away a slice of ownership and future profits, forever.

Usually best

Borrow with bonds

Get the cash now, pay a known amount of interest, and owe nothing once it's paid back. No ownership lost.

How you make money

Two ways a bond pays you

Way 1 · Income

Collect the coupons

Hold the bond to the end. You get $50 each year, then your $1,000 back. Steady and predictable.

Way 2 · Price change

Sell it to someone else

Bonds trade like used cars. If you sell for more than you paid, you keep the difference. You can also lose if you sell for less.

The rule that confuses everyone

Interest rates and bond prices sit on a seesaw

When new bonds start paying more, your old bond's fixed $50 looks worse. Nobody pays full price for it, so its price drops. When new bonds pay less, your old bond looks great, and its price rises.

Yield in 3 minutes

Yield is what you really earn

The coupon is fixed in dollars. The price you pay isn't. Yield turns both into one number you can compare.

Current yield=yearly coupon÷price you pay
Bought at a discount

$50 ÷ $900 = 5.6%

Paid less than face value, so you earn more than the coupon.

Bought at par

$50 ÷ $1,000 = 5.0%

Paid exactly face value. Yield equals the coupon.

Bought at a premium

$50 ÷ $1,100 = 4.5%

Paid extra, so you earn less than the coupon.

Price up, yield down. Price down, yield up. Same seesaw, different name. Pros usually quote yield to maturity, which also counts the gain or loss you lock in when you're paid back at face value.

Wall Street's favorite recession signal

Line up a government's bonds from shortest to longest and plot their yields. Normally, locking money away longer pays more, so the line slopes up.

When things go wrong

How a bond crash spreads

Bonds are the base layer of the financial system. When their prices fall hard, the shock travels.

Shock

Yields jump

Inflation fears or worry about a borrower's debt.

→
Losses

Bond prices fall

Banks, pensions and funds holding bonds lose value.

→
Squeeze

Borrowing gets pricey

Loans and mortgages cost more; lenders get cautious.

→
Slowdown

Economy cools

Less spending, less hiring, lower stock prices.

The big picture

Every bond yield is a price on three things

Time

How long you wait

Longer loans usually pay more, and their prices swing harder when rates move.

Risk

Will you get paid?

Shakier borrowers must pay higher yields to convince anyone to lend.

Trust

Do people believe the promise?

Confidence in a government or company keeps its borrowing cheap. Lose it, and costs jump.

Cheat sheet

Bond words at a glance

Bond
A loan you give to a government or company, written as an IOU.
Issuer
Whoever borrows the money and owes it back.
Face value (par)
The amount repaid at the end, often $1,000.
Coupon
Fixed yearly interest, as a percent of face value.
Maturity
The date the loan ends and face value is repaid.
Yield
Your real return, based on the price you actually paid.
Discount / premium
Trading below / above face value.
Treasury
A bond issued by the US government; the safest benchmark.
Yield curve
Yields plotted from short to long maturities.
Inverted curve
Short-term pays more than long-term; often a warning sign.
Credit rating
A grade (AAA to D) for how likely the borrower pays back.
Default
When the borrower fails to pay what it promised.
